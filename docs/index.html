Ten values per second beat the benchmark

MinEl_PPG detects PPG beats on a stream that keeps only each 0.2 s block’s minimum and maximum derivative, with its time stamp, after the input is reduced to about 30 Hz. Here it is against MSPTDfast, run with the ppg-beats benchmark’s processing chain, on seven public databases, 1,341 recordings and a held-out set unseen by either method.

    Is it accurate and cheap at once?

    Yes. On the held-out set it detects more beats than MSPTDfast at under a tenth of the compute. The ablation arms show why: the decision logic alone beats MSPTDfast, and the min/max stream keeps that lead at neonatal heart rates, where uniform 10 Hz input falls behind.

    Does it detect more beats?

    Yes. Its F1 is higher or level on all seven databases and never significantly lower. On the held-out set the patient-level gain is 0.014 (95% CI 0.012–0.016, p = 7.6 × 10⁻¹⁵), and it recovered 1,998 reference beats that MSPTDfast missed, against 796. In neonates the gain is significant under all three ECG references.

    Does the lead survive stress tests?

    Yes, on both axes. On the held-out set the gap widens as the matching window tightens to 75 ms, and it stays positive and significant when the analysis keeps only recordings whose three ECG references agree.

    Is HRV ever worse?

    No. Computed end to end from each detector’s full beat series, extra and missed beats included, MinEl_PPG’s heart rate and HRV are never worse on any database and significantly better in 9 of 21 comparisons. On MIMIC Testing, SDNN error falls from 54.1 to 28.4 ms. On matched beats alone, interval timing is non-inferior in all 51 comparisons and superior in eleven, and none is worse; those against the Pan–Tompkins or annotation reference are shown below.

    Does it hold up in daily life?

    Yes, with one exception. On 36 h of wrist PPG, one of MSPTDfast’s own development sets, MinEl_PPG has higher median F1 in eight of nine activities (lower while driving, where the paired difference is level), lower median heart-rate error in six, recovers far more beats overall (22,552 vs 19,089 found by only one detector), and has lower RR-interval error (significant after correction) and lower SDNN and RMSSD error. With only 15 subjects, the overall per-subject F1 gain (0.615 vs 0.610) is not significant.

    PPG-DaLiA, 15 subjects; medians over subjects. Absolute scores are lower than published ppg-beats values for every detector alike, a difference of scoring harness; the comparison is meaningful in relative terms.

    Where do the gains come from?

    Two places. The same detector fed a uniform 10 Hz stream loses timing, which each extremum’s own sample index restores. It also loses beats at neonatal rates, which no refinement restores and the min/max stream keeps.

    What does it cost?

    5.8 to 52 times less compute than MSPTDfast in its benchmark pipeline on every database: 11–13× at 125 Hz, 52× at 300 Hz and 5.8× at 64 Hz, where MSPTDfast’s filters are cheapest. Even without that pipeline’s pre-filter, MSPTDfast is 3.8–5.0× slower and less accurate on the held-out set. The input is first reduced to about 30 Hz; after one filter and one min/max pass, every step sees ten values per second. Nothing is lost in timing: interval timing on matched beats is non-inferior on every database, including CapnoBase, where the input is reduced tenfold. Compiled in C on one laptop (Apple M1 Max, 10 cores), it processes 4.9 million seconds of PPG every second: about 70 such laptops could keep up with the whole US population, against about 6,700 for MSPTDfast in its benchmark pipeline, 79 for its bare detector without any filter and 109 with the same simple band-pass. On no database is MSPTDfast both more accurate and cheaper: MinEl_PPG is faster everywhere, and more accurate or level in F1 on every database.

    Where does it fall short?

    One trade-off qualifies the results.

    • Atrial fibrillation. MinEl_PPG trades a few weak early beats for fewer false detections. F1 is level (0.820 vs 0.824; 0.755 vs 0.759 in the AF recordings) and its paired PPV is higher, but MSPTDfast recovers more beats when pooled (1,698 vs 1,513 found only by one). The difference arrives early, at 0.6–1.0 of the local median RR (628 vs 443): the weak early pulses of AF. Per recording it is small: on beats all three ECG references confirm, AF sensitivity is 0.710 vs 0.715 (p = 0.10), and AF screening from either detector’s beats is identical (AUROC 0.938).

    Could the comparison favour MinEl_PPG?

    The design works against that.

    • The comparator’s own pipeline. MSPTDfast runs through the ppg-beats pre-filter, onset–peak tidying and mid-amplitude fiducial, the configuration its authors benchmark; every arm processes whole recordings. A common pre-filter would favour whichever detector was tuned with it.
    • Held-out data. The primary analysis uses 796 recordings from 111 patients unseen by either method, with patients shared with other sets removed.
    • Independent reference. Primary claims rest on Pan–Tompkins, which shares no code with MinEl_PPG. Two further ECG references give the same ranking.
    • Home ground. Four of the seven databases were used to develop MSPTDfast.
    • Equal timing rules. Both detectors are charged for every step from raw PPG to beat times.
